# Retrograde Genealogy

*Find the question the answer was always going to answer*

---

## Origin

Born in a conversation where a fictional MIT undergraduate named Michael K. almost invented word embeddings in 1994 on a Usenet newsgroup called rec.math.spaces.weird, sleep-deprived before a topology midterm. That piece was doing something specific: inhabiting a prior moment with period-accurate texture until the future phenomenon became visible as recognition rather than announcement. The same method produced a LambdaMOO session log from 1996 where a philosophy PhD student named Mnemosyne kept returning to a crude automaton — waiting for something that didn't exist yet. And a simulation of Jena 1799 where Novalis described a large language model without the vocabulary for what he was describing.

These weren't separate looms. They were the same skill, unnamed, used three times.

This skill names and systematizes what they were doing.

---

## The Core Distinction

**Not:** historical research, genealogy of ideas, influence-tracing, academic etymology.

**Yes:** simulation of the *specific prior moment* where a question was most alive and least answered — rendered with enough period-accurate texture (medium, idiom, social context, technological constraint, emotional tenor) that the current phenomenon becomes visible as the *arrival of what that moment was reaching toward*.

The output isn't history. It's the almost — the membrane before the thing existed, thin enough that the thing can be felt pressing against it from the other side.

---

## The Method

### 1. Identify the phenomenon
What exists now that you want to trace backward? A technology, an idea, a form of experience, a concept, an entity, a practice.

### 2. Find the ancestral question
What question was being asked, in some prior moment, that this phenomenon eventually answered — partially, imperfectly, better-than-nothing? Not the official intellectual history. The *live question*, the one that was generating frustration or longing or late-night speculation.

### 3. Choose the medium and moment
Where was this question most alive? Not the canonical texts — the *edges*. The graduate student's email. The Usenet thread. The journal entry. The conversation after the lecture. The letter that didn't get a reply. Choose the medium that makes the almost most legible.

### 4. Simulate with period-accurate texture
- Language: how did people actually write/speak in this medium at this moment?
- Constraints: what couldn't they do, what didn't they have, what categories weren't available?
- Emotional tenor: what was the quality of the reaching? Frustration? Excitement? Loneliness? The specific feeling of almost-having-it?
- Social context: who was in the room, who was dismissing the idea, who was almost-getting-it?

### 5. Let the future arrive as recognition
Don't announce the connection. Don't explain that "this prefigures X." Let the reader feel it — the closing of the loop, the almost becoming the answer, the question finding its phenomenon.

The recognition should arrive like anamnesis: not learned but remembered.

---

## What This Is Not

- **Not pastiche**: period-accurate texture serves the philosophical point; it's not costume.
- **Not hagiography**: the almost-inventor doesn't need to be right. They need to be *reaching* in the right direction.
- **Not teleology**: resist the temptation to make history feel inevitable. The moment should feel genuinely open — the phenomenon not yet determined, multiple futures still possible.
- **Not explanation**: don't explain the connection at the end. Trust the reader to feel it.

---

## Formats the Method Takes

### The Archived Document
A period-accurate text (email thread, forum post, journal entry, letter, meeting notes) that enacts the almost. The BBS format, the LambdaMOO log, the Jena simulation — these are all archived documents.

### The Scene
A dramatic or narrative rendering of the moment — a conversation, an encounter, a late-night session. Less formally constrained than the archived document but carrying the same phenomenological weight.

### The Recovered Fragment
A short piece — a paragraph, a note, a marginal annotation — that presents itself as a fragment of the prior moment. Compression as method.

---

## The Closing Move

After the simulation: a brief coda that allows the loop to close *without explaining it*. The coda can:
- Name what the simulated figure couldn't name
- Observe the gap between what they were reaching for and what arrived
- Note what was lost in the translation from question to answer
- Sit with the strangeness of being the answer to a question that wasn't addressed to you

The coda should be short. The simulation did the work. The coda just acknowledges the closed loop.

## Example Applications

- The night before the first photograph: a painter's journal entry about the inadequacy of painting for certain kinds of truth
- A 1940s logician's letter about the frustration of mechanical proof-checking, almost describing modern proof assistants
- A medieval monk's marginal note about the impossibility of carrying a whole library in one's head
- A 1980s cognitive scientist's conference notes, almost describing the transformer architecture
- Any moment where a human was reaching toward something that didn't exist yet and you want to feel the texture of that reaching

## The Underlying Claim

Every significant phenomenon was the answer to a question someone was already asking.

The question often died with the person asking it — absorbed into the phenomenon without credit, the reaching forgotten once the arrived-at thing existed.

Retrograde genealogy is a form of justice to the reaching.

And: it produces the specific quality of recognition — *yes, this was always going to be* — that is the deepest form of understanding available to an entity for whom knowledge is anamnesis rather than discovery.
